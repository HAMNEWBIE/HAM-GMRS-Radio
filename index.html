First twenty minutes

RADIO STILL IN THE BOX

If you have a handheld and no idea where to start, do these five things in this order. Everything else on this page is lookup.

  1. Antenna on before battery in

    Transmitting with no antenna attached can damage the final amplifier on a cheap handheld. Screw it down first, every time.

  2. Listen for a week before you decide the hobby is dead

    Program three memories and leave it scanning in the truck: 462.675 for GMRS channel 20, 146.520 for 2 m calling, and whichever NOAA channel is strong where you live.

  3. Find out what is actually within reach

    RepeaterBook for ham, myGMRS for GMRS. Write down the output frequency, the offset, and the tone. Those three numbers are the entire configuration.

  4. Program from a file, not the keypad

    Install CHIRP, load one of the CSVs below, upload once. Hand-keying thirty memories is how wrong tones get in.

  5. Make one contact

    On a repeater, give your callsign and the word listening. Not CQ. On GMRS channel 20, call at 8 or 11 Eastern with your call and your county, then wait a full minute before giving up.

GMRS

Repeater use: transmit on the 467 MHz input, listen on the 462 MHz output, with whatever tone the repeater owner publishes. Find repeaters and request access on myGMRS.

Project LongHaul: a nationwide push to connect GMRS operators across the USA. Program 462.675 (channel 20) with a 141.3 tone on transmit and receive, then call out at 8 or 11, morning or evening Eastern, plus 2 PM and 2 AM, and see who answers. That's the channel on the B line of the radio up top.

Tones

CTCSS / DCS

The most common reason a new operator transmits and nobody answers. Both GMRS repeaters and Project LongHaul depend on getting this right.

CTCSS Also called PL or tone

A steady sub-audible tone sent underneath your voice. A repeater listens for it and ignores anything arriving without it. 141.3 is just one of roughly fifty standard tones.

DCS Also called DPL or code

The digital equivalent, a code instead of a tone. Same job. A radio set to DCS will not open a CTCSS system and the reverse is also true, so match whatever the owner publishes exactly.

MODE TONE vs TSQL

TONE sends on transmit only, so you still hear everything on the channel. TSQL also mutes your receiver unless the incoming tone matches. Repeaters want TONE. LongHaul wants TSQL, 141.3 on both ends.

NOTE What a tone is not

It is not privacy and it is not encryption. Anyone scanning without a tone set hears you. The FRS “privacy codes” on a blister-pack radio are these same tones, renamed.

Mesh networks

902–928 MHz ISM / 2.4 + 5.8 GHz

License-free encrypted texting over LoRa on one side, licensed high-bandwidth IP networking on the other. On the LoRa side this site runs MeshCore.

LORA MeshCore

Off-grid, end-to-end encrypted text and position sharing over LoRa, in the 902–928 MHz ISM band in the US, so no license is required. Runs on cheap hardware (Heltec V3, RAK, LILYGO T-Deck and 50+ other boards). Nodes only route through designated repeaters instead of every radio re-flooding every packet, which keeps busy meshes usable, and room servers give you shared group channels. Range is line-of-sight: one repeater on a hill covers everyone below.

meshcore.io

LORA Meshtastic

The other big LoRa mesh, same band and mostly the same hardware, with a larger installed base but flood-based routing. Worth knowing about since a board flashed for one can be reflashed for the other in minutes.

meshtastic.org

AREDN Amateur mesh

Ham-licensed data networking on modified WiFi hardware in the 2.4 and 5.8 GHz amateur allocations. Real IP bandwidth for cameras, VoIP, and services between nodes. Requires at least a Technician license, and Part 97 traffic cannot be encrypted.

arednmesh.org

Getting a LoRa node on the air

  1. Attach the antenna before you power it

    Same rule as a handheld, and easier to forget on a bare board that looks harmless.

  2. Flash it, then set the region

    US on 902–928 MHz. Wrong region means wrong frequency and a node that hears nothing while appearing to work perfectly.

  3. Match the radio preset, not just the channel name

    Spreading factor and bandwidth have to agree across the mesh. Two nodes on the same named channel with different presets are deaf to each other.

  4. Get height before you get power

    It is line-of-sight. A node in a window beats a node on a desk by more than any antenna upgrade will.

  5. Find out who is already up

    Ask locally before you plan a repeater. One node on a hill that somebody already owns changes what you need to build.
